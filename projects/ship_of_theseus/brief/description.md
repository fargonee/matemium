# The Ship of Theseus as an Argument Map

## Project identity

- **Subject:** Philosophy
- **Project slug:** `ship_of_theseus`
- **Status:** First authored prototype complete; engine feedback recorded; flagship render not yet accepted
- **Central question:** What makes an object remain the same object while its parts change?
- **Primary audience:** General learners with no philosophy background
- **Target format:** 16:9 flagship master, approximately 2–3 minutes

## Purpose

This project must visualize philosophical reasoning rather than merely illustrate the familiar ship story. It should expose claims, intuitions, objections, counterexamples, and unresolved tradeoffs in a navigable argument map.

The viewer should finish with clearer distinctions and better questions, not a falsely authoritative answer.

## Learning outcomes

The viewer should understand:

- the difference between numerical identity and qualitative similarity;
- how gradual replacement motivates continuity-based intuitions;
- how reconstructing the removed parts creates a competing claimant;
- why material, continuity, form, function, and history support different answers;
- how a counterexample tests a principle;
- why philosophical progress can consist of refining concepts rather than reaching immediate agreement.

## Narrative arc

1. **The intuition:** Replace one damaged plank and ask whether the ship remains the same.
2. **Gradual continuation:** Repeat replacements over time while a continuity indicator remains stable.
3. **The challenge:** Reveal that all original planks were preserved.
4. **Reconstruction:** Assemble a second ship and ask which, if either, is the original.
5. **Argument map:** Branch into material continuity, spatiotemporal continuity, form/function, and historical-causal accounts.
6. **Test each account:** Attach one concise supporting intuition and one objection to each.
7. **Clarify vocabulary:** Separate “same properties” from “the very same object.”
8. **Transfer:** Briefly apply the structure to a repaired body, organization, or digital file.
9. **Synthesis:** Leave the branches visible and frame the unresolved choice as a conflict among criteria.

## Visual and motion direction

- Use the ship as a stable spatial anchor while the argument structure develops around it.
- Distinguish claims, evidence, objections, and questions by shape as well as color.
- Animate conceptual relationships directionally.
- Keep the argument map readable as a whole; use camera focus for local branches.
- Avoid portraits of philosophers unless historically necessary.
- Use neutral language and balanced visual weight among serious positions.

## Matemium capabilities this project must demonstrate

- argument and concept mapping;
- branching reasoning;
- object-state transformation over time;
- camera navigation across a large conceptual canvas;
- objections linked to precise claims;
- comparison and transfer of abstract criteria;
- structured claim data separated from rendering code.

## Required source and assets

- Build the ship and replacement sequence from procedural simplified shapes.
- Represent the argument as explicit nodes and typed edges.
- Include short definitions for central terms in project data.
- Require no external media or network access.

## Scope boundaries

- Do not present one theory as the official solution.
- Do not confuse legal ownership, naming convention, and metaphysical identity.
- Do not overload the map with every historical treatment of identity.
- Do not use the ship animation without connecting it to explicit premises.
- Avoid treating objections as jokes or visually inferior branches.

## Acceptance criteria

- Every conclusion is visibly connected to its supporting criterion.
- At least four accounts receive both a motivation and a meaningful objection.
- The reconstructed-plank challenge is spatially and conceptually clear.
- Terminology remains understandable without sacrificing accuracy.
- The final frame preserves genuine tension rather than announcing a winner.
- The result demonstrates Matemium’s ability to animate structured thought itself.
